Brain and Society

Understanding how learning and living together work. We come directly into primary school and work with whole classes. In age-appropriate, interactive lessons, children gain an insight into current findings from research on learning, development and the brain.

What it is about

Children find out how learning works, what matters for getting along well together, and what part artificial intelligence plays in their everyday lives. Our aim is to strengthen children in understanding their own learning better, in building social competence, and in finding their way safely and responsibly in an increasingly digital world.

How learning works

Children often experience learning as something that either succeeds or fails. Research on learning shows, however, that development is a process: competence comes from practice, persistence, and the readiness to take on something new. Together with the children we look at how learning happens in the brain and why mistakes are an important part of every process of learning. They find that difficulty is not a sign of lacking talent, but an indication of where development can happen. They also find that good learning often feels slow and effortful, while what goes down easily and quickly is just as quickly gone. Out of this grows an understanding of learning that gives courage.

How getting along well works

Learning and social relationships are closely bound together. Children spend a large part of their day in groups, where they experience friendship, conflict, cooperation and belonging. The development of social and emotional competence is therefore an important foundation for successful learning. The American neuroscientist Matthew Lieberman describes the need to belong as a fundamental human need. Children therefore work on questions such as: how do we speak to one another with respect? How do we deal with conflict? How can we understand others and be understood ourselves? Together, strategies are developed for meeting disagreement constructively. Children find that conflict is part of living together, and that relationships can be strengthened through communication, understanding and mutual respect.

What a machine can do, and what it cannot

Artificial intelligence will accompany children throughout their education and their working lives. It is all the more important to develop a realistic and differentiated understanding of this technology early on. Together we look at which tasks artificial intelligence solves well, where its limits lie, and why human capacities remain indispensable. Children learn to use technology critically and reflectively, and to distinguish between information, support, and a real human relationship. What becomes clear is this: machines can process information and deliver answers. But they can neither take responsibility nor shape a real relationship. Empathy, compassion, trust and human connection remain human capacities.

Capacities for the future

Brain and Society does not only give children knowledge about learning, community and technology. The lessons build important future capacities at the same time: self-reflection, communication, empathy, critical thinking, and a responsible way of using digital tools. Children experience themselves not as passive listeners but as active shapers of their learning and of their life together. They develop a deeper understanding of themselves, of other people, and of the world they are growing up in.

A boy is working on a word problem and asks an artificial intelligence to explain a solution. The answer sounds convincing, but it contains a flaw in reasoning. Together with the class, the solution is examined and checked step by step. In doing so the children discover that not every answer is automatically right, not even when it sounds very sure of itself.

Understanding rather than simply adopting

Experiences like this one are at the heart of Brain and Society. Children learn to question information critically, to notice their own thinking, and to take responsibility for their learning. They find that technological tools can help, but that understanding only comes about when people ask questions, see how things connect, and reflect on what they find together. In this way they develop confidence, critical thinking, and the ability to use digital technology competently and responsibly: artificial intelligence can support, but the real educational work of understanding, judging and placing knowledge stays with people.
