Brain and Society

Understanding how learning and living together work. We are developing visits to primary schools, working with whole classes. In age-appropriate, interactive lessons, children gain insight into current findings from research on learning, development and the brain.

What it is about

Children explore how learning works, what matters for getting along well together and what part artificial intelligence plays in everyday life. Our aim is to help children understand their own learning better, build social competence and find their way safely and responsibly in an increasingly digital world.

How learning works

Children often experience learning as something that either succeeds or fails. Research on learning shows, however, that development is a process: competence comes from practice, persistence and the readiness to meet new challenges. Together with the children, we look at how learning happens in the brain and why mistakes are an essential part of every learning process. They also discover that durable learning often feels slow and effortful, whereas what passes effortlessly tends to fade just as quickly.

How getting along well works

Learning and social relationships are closely bound together. Children spend a large part of their day in groups where they experience friendship, conflict, cooperation and belonging. Neuroscientist Matthew Lieberman describes the need to belong as a fundamental human need. Together, children work on practical questions: how do we speak to one another with respect, how do we meet disagreement constructively, and how do we understand others and be understood ourselves?

What a machine can do, and what it cannot

Artificial intelligence will accompany children throughout their education and working lives, making an early, realistic understanding essential. Together we examine which tasks AI handles well, where its limits lie and why human capacities remain indispensable. Machines can process information and generate answers; taking responsibility and sustaining genuine relationships remain human.

Capacities for the future

Brain and Society builds more than factual knowledge about learning, community and technology. The lessons strengthen self-reflection, communication, empathy, critical thinking and responsible use of digital tools, inviting children to experience themselves as active shapers of their own learning.

An illustrative example A boy is working on a word problem and asks an artificial intelligence to explain a solution. The answer sounds convincing, yet contains a flaw in reasoning. Together with the class, the solution is examined step by step, and the children discover that not every answer is automatically right, even when it sounds completely sure of itself.

Understanding rather than simply adopting

Experiences like this one sit at the heart of Brain and Society. Children learn to question information critically, notice their own thinking and take responsibility for their learning. Artificial intelligence can support, yet the real educational work of understanding, judging and placing knowledge remains with people.
