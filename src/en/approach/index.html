Our approach

How we work.

A child learns deeply when two things come together: they feel their own effort moving them forward, and they know someone who truly knows them stays alongside them and trusts what they can do.

A child stays with it when progress feels real

Learning asks for persistence. Confidence grows when a child experiences directly that their own thinking and practice make a difference. Informed by research on learning and engagement (including Lu et al., 2025, where experienced learning progress during adult task sessions was associated with deeper absorption and lower distraction), we organise our work around making growth visible.

In practice, that means naming concrete steps honestly, looking back together at what felt difficult a few weeks earlier, and deciding with the child what to take on next.

Just within reach

Tasks that are too easy bore a child; tasks that are too hard discourage them. Together with each child, we look for the zone between the two: challenges that invite genuine thought without overwhelming them.

In one-to-one or small-group work, that calibration happens continuously. A mistake is not a verdict; it is a clear clue showing where understanding needs another bridge. Lasting comprehension comes through trying, explaining in one’s own words and revising.

Someone who stays

Trust cannot be rushed. It grows through reliability. As Isabelle C. Hau (Executive Director of the Stanford Accelerator for Learning) writes, caring relationships are the foundation of learning. Developmental psychologist Susan Engel likewise documents how strongly children’s curiosity in classrooms depends on whether adults notice and follow up on their questions.

Longitudinal studies point in the same direction from complementary angles: Hamre and Pianta (2001, pp. 631–632) followed 179 children through eighth grade and found substantial associations between early kindergarten teacher-child relationships and later academic outcomes. At the level of whole schools, Anthony S. Bryk (2010) linked sustained gains in reading and mathematics in Chicago schools to relational trust among school leaders, teachers, parents and communities. Both studies document empirical associations rather than simple cause-and-effect mechanisms, and together they provide strong grounding for relationship-centred education.

That is why we commit to continuity: at Lernbus, the same child meets the same learning mentor every week across a full semester and often longer. That person remembers the open question from last week and counts on what the child can achieve next.

Human guidance in the age of AI

Digital tools and artificial intelligence are part of the world children are growing up in. They can offer explanations, generate practice variations or spark ideas. An exploratory study by Chin et al. (2024) with eight five- and six-year-olds noted, for instance, that a language model gave more frequent positive remarks during structured storytelling than parents in the same setting.

Yet encouragement from software does not constitute a relationship. A machine can generate fluent answers, but it cannot truly know a child, bear pedagogical responsibility or stay reliably alongside someone through difficulty. Those human capacities sit at the centre of our work.

From principle to practice

Our work draws on principles of the Reggio Emilia tradition: children are active shapers of their own learning, and their paths of discovery deserve visibility and respect. This shared foundation guides every initiative of the association.

Through Lernbus at Münsterplatz 17, we accompany children weekly, individually or in small groups, over the long term and in close contact with families, kindergarten and school. With our planned Forscherbühne, we draw on the tradition of public science demonstrations for young audiences pioneered by the Royal Institution’s Christmas Lectures in London since 1825. And with Brain and Society, we are developing interactive classroom lessons exploring how learning works, how groups live well together and where artificial intelligence fits.

Readers interested in a deeper philosophical account can explore Jonas Hertner’s standalone 2026 essay, A Manifesto for Relational Learning.

Every child wants to understand the world and to be understood in their questions.
