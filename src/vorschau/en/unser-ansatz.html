How we work

How we work

A child learns best when two things come together. They feel themselves moving forward. And they know there is a person who knows them and believes they can do this. Everything we do is built from these two things.

A child stays with it when they feel themselves getting better

A child stays with something when they notice they are making progress. Every small step they see in themselves gives them the courage for the next one. Neuroscience from Erasmus University Rotterdam shows that we are most deeply absorbed, and least easily distracted, when we feel we are moving forward. This drive needs no reward from outside, the progress itself makes concentrating feel good. And it works over longer arcs, not from one moment to the next. So staying with it is worth far more than a quick try.

Just within reach

What a child likes best is whatever is just within reach, not too easy and not too hard. That is no accident: the same research shows that learning progress is greatest at medium difficulty. So people stay longest with tasks that stretch them just the right amount. A quick, kind answer shows the child what comes next. A mistake here is not a failure, it shows where the way leads on. And what a child tries out for themselves, they truly understand.

Someone who stays

Trust grows slowly, and only with people who stay. When a child feels that someone really knows them, they dare to do more. Isabelle Hau, executive director of the Stanford Accelerator for Learning, puts it simply: relationships are the foundation of learning. Close, sustaining relationships in the first years of school predict better results years later, even once family background and IQ are taken into account. A large study of Chicago schools found that schools with a great deal of mutual trust improved in reading and mathematics ten times as often as schools with little. That is why, with us, the same child comes to the same person every week, for a whole term and often longer. That person thinks about the child between the sessions and brings back the question that was left open last week. The loveliest feeling for a child is that someone is counting on them and believes they can manage.

What a machine cannot do

Children today receive praise and affirmation far more often from machines than from people, by some estimates thirteen times as often. So the more screens there are around a child, the more the person beside them matters. A screen can praise a child without end, but it cannot believe in them. It does not notice when a child is sad or having a hard day. To find your way back to someone after a quarrel, a child can only learn that with a person. And a child who is only ever praised never learns to handle a disappointment, and that is exactly what a child needs in life.

Three pillars, one aim

The Forscherbühne awakens wonder and turns real science into something you can experience, for children and young people in Basel. The Learning Bus goes with each child at their own pace, alongside someone who stays. Brain and Society brings into the classroom how learning, and getting along well together, can work, and what a machine can and cannot do. In all of this we work in the tradition of Reggio Emilia: the child is the protagonist of their own learning, and what they learn is made visible. Three paths, one aim: that a child learns to understand, and feels understood.

Every child wants to understand, and to be understood.
